    About the Apocrypha

    What is the Deuterocanon?

    The Deuterocanon, also called the Apocrypha, is a set of Jewish writings from roughly 400–50 BC, the period between the Old and New Testaments. It includes books such as Tobit, Judith, 1 & 2 Maccabees, Sirach, and Wisdom of Solomon.

    Who accepts them as Scripture?

    Catholic and Orthodox Christians consider these books canonical. Most Protestants do not, following the Hebrew Bible’s narrower canon. The books were never cited as Scripture by Jesus or the Apostles, and some passages are difficult to reconcile with the rest of Scripture, for example prayers for the dead in 2 Maccabees 12.

    What did the Protestant Reformers say?

    Martin Luther (1534): “Apocrypha, that is, books not considered equal to Holy Scripture, but which are still useful and good to read.”
    Thirty-Nine Articles (1563): “The other books the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine.”
    Belgic Confession (1561): “The church may certainly read these books and learn from them as far as they agree with the canonical books.”

    Why are they here?

    They are included for study and historical interest. These writings shaped Jewish thought in the centuries before Christ and appear in translations used by billions of Christians worldwide.
